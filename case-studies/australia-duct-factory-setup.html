SBAL-III Line for Australian Sheet Metal Workshop

How a Brisbane-based sheet metal workshop replaced a mix of individual manual forming machines with a coordinated SBAL-III auto duct line — covering the full process from factory planning and import logistics through to commissioning and first production run.

Machine: SBAL-III Rectangular Duct GI + Zincalume 0.5–1.0mm TDF + Pittsburgh Australia

Production figures and estimates in this case study are based on the buyer's reported outcomes for their specific factory configuration and product mix. Results will vary depending on shift patterns, operator experience, material quality, and local market conditions.

Situation Before: The Manual Machine Mix Problem

The workshop was a second-generation sheet metal business in the Brisbane industrial belt, producing rectangular HVAC duct for residential and light commercial projects. The operation was built on individual machines purchased at different times from different suppliers — a standalone plasma cutter, a floor-standing folder, a second-hand Pittsburgh lock former, and a manual notching station.

The core problems with this setup:

  • Coordination bottlenecks: Output at each machine station was paced differently. The folder was the slowest element — one operator handling a single panel at a time — creating a queue of cut blanks waiting for forming that disrupted workshop flow.
  • Labour intensity at volume: At their target of 500–600 m²/day, the existing setup required 7–8 workers across all stations and still hit quality inconsistency on Pittsburgh lock tightness — a common field complaint from their installation crews.
  • Material handling waste: Manual panel movement between stations generated 3–5% handling damage on thinner gauges (0.5–0.6mm Zincalume). This was showing up in finished duct rejection rates during site delivery inspections.

The owner's decision point was a large commercial project — 18,000 m² of rectangular duct supply for a logistics centre — that required consistent delivery of 800+ m²/day over a 14-week period. The manual setup could not reliably sustain that output. The choice was either subcontract the overflow or upgrade the line.

Why SBAL-III Over SBAL-V

The SBAL-V produces 800–2,500 m²/day and is the higher-output configuration. The buyer's calculation was that their normal weekly volume — excluding the logistics centre project — was 400–600 m²/day. Running an SBAL-V at 30–40% of capacity for most of the year was not economically optimal. The SBAL-III was specified at the same sheet width (up to 1,250mm) with a lower capital cost and a production range that matched their realistic operating requirements.

Import and Logistics Process

This was the buyer's first direct machinery import from China. Ryan in Melbourne handled the commercial and logistics coordination.

Key steps in the import process:

  1. Proforma invoice issued; payment via T/T 30% deposit, 70% on B/L copy
  2. Machine manufactured and tested at SBKJ factory — 45-day production schedule
  3. Pre-shipment video sent to buyer showing test run on 0.8mm GI
  4. FCL container from Nanjing port to Brisbane; transit 22 days
  5. Customs clearance via buyer's freight forwarder using HS code 8462.21 (bending machines)
  6. Delivery to factory floor via flatbed truck; unloading with hired 10T forklift

Import duty rate applicable at the time of purchase was 0% for this machinery category under Australia's unilateral preference schedule. GST was applicable and claimed as input credit. The buyer confirmed with their accountant before purchase.

Factory Layout and Site Preparation

The SBAL-III requires a clear run of approximately 22 metres for the full line including coil unwind stand and output table. The Brisbane workshop had a 28m × 18m bay available — adequate but not spacious.

Preparation completed before machine arrival:

  • Concrete floor survey — confirmed 150mm slab adequate for machine weight
  • Three-phase 380V/50Hz power feed to line position (Australian standard: 415V; transformer supplied with machine)
  • Compressed air feed — 0.6 MPa, 200 L/min minimum — connected from existing compressor
  • Overhead crane track extended to cover the full line length for coil loading
  • Former manual machine stations decommissioned and removed

The 415V/380V voltage step-down transformer was included in the SBKJ supply. This is standard for Australian buyers — Ryan confirms this during specification so it does not arrive as a surprise cost.

Commissioning

SBKJ sent one commissioning engineer who flew from China to Brisbane. Visa processing (business visa, 3-month) was handled by the buyer's company as a condition of commissioning — standard practice for Australian installations.

Commissioning ran over 8 days:

  • Days 1–2: Line assembly, power connection, first motion check (no material)
  • Day 3: First test run — 0.8mm GI coil, 1,000mm sheet width, 400×200mm duct size
  • Days 4–5: CNC program entry for 24 standard duct sizes used by the buyer's projects; operator instruction on parameter input, speed adjustment, and blade replacement procedure
  • Days 6–7: Zincalume run — 0.6mm and 0.7mm gauges; Pittsburgh lock quality check across the output range; TDF flange dimension verification against AS1668 specifications
  • Day 8: Full production shift — buyer's operators running the line independently; engineer observing only
