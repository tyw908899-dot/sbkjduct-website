Stadium and Sports Venue HVAC Duct Guide — Olympic Brisbane 2032, FIFA World Cup, Bowl Ventilation, NFPA 92 Smoke Management

A specification-grade engineering guide to stadium and sports venue HVAC ductwork — bowl ventilation strategy, premium suite air handling, NFPA 92 smoke management, retractable roof mode interlocks, NFPA 96 commercial kitchen exhaust, locker room dehumidification and Olympic-grade BMS sequencing. Written for stadium architects, sports venue HVAC consultants, mechanical contractors and facility engineers preparing for Brisbane 2032, FIFA World Cup 2026, FIFA 2030, FIFA 2034 and the wider major-event upgrade cycle.

By SBKJ Engineering Team · Published May 4, 2026 · 32 min read · Reviewed by SBKJ QA & Engineering

Why stadium HVAC is unique

Stadium HVAC is not a scaled-up office building. The combination of 60,000 to 100,000 seat occupancy, peak loads concentrated into 4-hour event windows, retractable roof variability, and the mix of bowl, premium suite, concourse, locker room and back-of-house in a single envelope makes stadium ventilation one of the most demanding subsets of building services engineering. A typical Premier League ground or AFL venue carries an event-day sensible cooling load 8 to 12 times higher than its idle-day baseline, and the supply duct network must handle that swing while keeping bowl noise below NC-30, suite noise below NC-25, and complying with NFPA 92 smoke management for assembly occupancy.

The economic stakes are equally large. A 60,000-seat stadium typically carries 80,000 to 140,000 m² of HVAC ductwork representing a capital cost of AUD 28 to 55 million on the duct package alone. Specifying that package wrong — wrong bowl strategy, wrong suite acoustic class, wrong smoke management approach, wrong fabrication tolerance — drives rework that compounds into the broader mechanical critical path. On a project running to a fixed Olympic or FIFA opening date, that rework lands directly on the practical-completion deadline. Brisbane 2032 has roughly 75 months of design and construction window from green-light to opening ceremony, and the Gabba redevelopment, Brisbane Live Arena and supporting venue upgrades will all consume the majority of that window on mechanical fitout alone.

This guide is the same engineering reference that SBKJ engineers walk through with mechanical contractors and consultants preparing for Olympic, FIFA and AFC-grade stadium projects. The first half explains the venue, code, and zone fundamentals. The second half walks through specification-grade detail by zone — bowl, suite, concourse, locker room, kitchen and smoke management — with the material, fabrication and acoustic specification needed to write a tender package that survives value engineering. The final sections cover Australian and global stadium project pipelines, machinery loadout for fabrication at scale, and a 20-step specification procedure aligned to ASHRAE, NFPA, AS 1668.2 and IBC.

Stadium and sports venue typology

Before any HVAC sizing, classify the venue. The seven most common typologies each carry different load profiles, code paths and duct material decisions.

  1. Outdoor open stadium. Open-air bowl with natural ventilation. HVAC concentrates on premium suites, concourse retail and food service, locker room, BOH, broadcast and corporate spaces. The bowl itself is unconditioned. MCG, Marvel Stadium, Adelaide Oval and Optus Stadium fall in this category. ASHRAE 62.1 still applies to the conditioned spaces but bowl smoke management is governed by natural-ventilation arguments under IBC 909.
  2. Retractable-roof stadium. The most demanding category from an HVAC perspective. Roof-open mode runs natural ventilation, roof-closed mode demands full mechanical conditioning of the bowl. Suncorp Stadium Brisbane (under expansion), Marvel Stadium Melbourne, NRG Stadium Houston, AT&T Stadium Arlington and several FIFA 2022 venues use this configuration. Roof position is a critical BMS interlock.
  3. Fully enclosed indoor arena. Permanent envelope, mechanical conditioning year-round. Used for basketball, ice hockey, indoor football and concert tours. Brisbane Live Arena, Sydney's planned indoor arena upgrades, Rod Laver Arena and Melbourne Park's John Cain Arena are examples. Loads are dominated by latent and sensible peaks during events with rapid recovery between sessions.
  4. Multi-purpose venue. Stadium configured to convert between sport, concert, exhibition and conference modes. The duct network must support 100% reconfiguration of seating and stage layouts without rework. Marvel Stadium and similar dual-purpose AFL/concert venues have this requirement.
  5. Training facility. Lower public occupancy but still demanding from a player-comfort and locker room perspective. Athletic department buildings at AFL clubs, NRL training grounds and football academy facilities run year-round on tighter margins than match-day venues.
  6. Sports medical and rehabilitation centre. Hospital-grade air filtration and pressure regimes for treatment rooms, hydrotherapy pools, MRI and imaging suites. Often co-located with elite training facilities. The HVAC overlap with healthcare facility design is significant.
  7. Olympic Village and athlete accommodation. Residential-grade HVAC at scale, with the legacy-mode requirement to convert to social or market-rate housing post-Games. Brisbane 2032's athlete village is being designed for this dual purpose from the outset.

Code framework — the standards that govern stadium HVAC

Stadium HVAC sits at the intersection of ventilation, energy, fire safety and acoustic codes. The seven documents below are the core reference set for any specification engineer.

  1. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 — Ventilation for Acceptable Indoor Air Quality. Table 6-1 occupancy classifications drive the breathing-zone outdoor air rate (Vbz) for every zone in the stadium. Spectator areas typically classify at 7.5 L/s/person; locker rooms at 2.5 L/s/m² plus exhaust; commercial kitchens governed by NFPA 96 referenced through 62.1. Demand-controlled ventilation is permitted for variable-occupancy spaces under Section 6.2.7 and is essential for stadium economics.
  2. ASHRAE Standard 90.1 — Energy Standard for Buildings. Drives envelope, lighting, fan power and equipment efficiency targets. Stadium HVAC fan power limits per Section 6.5.3 are non-trivial because long bowl supply runs accumulate static pressure rapidly. The 90.1 prescriptive path is rarely viable for stadium projects — most pursue the energy cost budget compliance path.
  3. AS 1668.2 — The use of ventilation and airconditioning in buildings. Australian equivalent to ASHRAE 62.1. Section 3 specifies ventilation rates for assembly occupancies. For Brisbane 2032, MCG, Marvel, Optus, Adelaide Oval and Gabba projects, AS 1668.2 governs and should be cross-checked against ASHRAE 62.1 — sometimes AS 1668.2 is more stringent on assembly occupancy.
  4. AS/NZS 4254 — Ductwork for air-handling systems in buildings. Australian fabrication, installation and testing standard. Class A, B and C leakage classifications align with EN 12237. Stadium bowl supply ducts typically specified Class A; BOH typically Class B. Read in conjunction with AS 4254 Parts 1 (rigid duct) and 2 (flexible duct).
  5. NFPA 92 — Standard for Smoke Control Systems. Governs bowl smoke purge, atrium smoke control, exhaust shaft sizing and stairwell pressurisation. For assembly occupancies above 1,000 occupants (every stadium qualifies), smoke management is mandatory in jurisdictions following NFPA. The Australian equivalent is AS 1668.1 read with the National Construction Code.
  6. NFPA 96 — Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations. Governs all stadium catering kitchen exhaust — Type I hood specification, grease duct continuous welded construction, cleanout access spacing, and automatic fire suppression integration. NFPA 96 is referenced through both NFPA 1 and the IBC and is enforced in most jurisdictions including Australia by reference.
  7. IBC and IFC — International Building Code and Fire Code. Assembly occupancy classifications under IBC Chapter 3, smoke management under IBC 909, fire damper and combination fire-smoke damper requirements under IBC 717. The IBC is the primary code path for US venues and is referenced in many international FIFA and IOC specifications.
  8. CIBSE TM52 — Limits of Thermal Comfort. Although a UK reference, TM52's adaptive thermal comfort framework is increasingly used on European and Middle East stadium projects to justify wider operational comfort bands than ASHRAE 55 strict criteria. Relevant for FIFA 2030 and 2034 venues.

Bowl ventilation — the largest single duct system in the building

The bowl is the spectator seating volume — the single largest occupied space in the venue and the dominant driver of total air-handling capacity. Three distribution strategies dominate modern stadium design, and the choice between them drives the bowl duct geometry, fascia plenum size and architectural integration.

Strategy 1 — perimeter slot-diffuser supply

Conditioned air is delivered from a perimeter plenum running along the upper edge of the seating tier, typically integrated into the underside of the roof structure or behind the upper concourse fascia. Slot diffusers throw air down the seating gradient at 6 to 10 m/s discharge velocity, decaying to under 0.5 m/s by the time it reaches occupants. The supply duct system is high-volume, moderately high-velocity, and routes around the bowl perimeter as a continuous loop or sectioned arc. This is the dominant strategy in open-roof and partially-enclosed bowls including MCG premium tier supply, Suncorp Stadium and many AFL venues. The duct mains are typically 1,200 to 2,000 mm rectangular galvanised G275 with 50 mm internal acoustic lining for the last 6 metres before the diffuser plenum.

Strategy 2 — under-seat displacement supply

Conditioned air is delivered at low velocity (under 0.25 m/s discharge) at floor level under each seat, rising naturally through the occupant breathing zone before exhausting at the upper bowl. This is the most acoustically benign and energy-efficient bowl ventilation strategy because it conditions only the occupied breathing zone, not the full bowl volume. Used in Allianz Arena Munich, several Qatar 2022 venues and is increasingly specified for fully-enclosed indoor arenas. The duct system runs from under-stand AHU farms up through a network of vertical risers to under-seat plenums, with substantial concrete-cast plenum construction integrated with the structural seating bowl. Galvanised G275 rectangular duct dominates the upstream risers.

Strategy 3 — hybrid bowl supply

Combines perimeter slot diffuser overhead supply with under-seat or aisle-edge displacement supply. Used in fully-enclosed indoor arenas where load profile and acoustic requirements demand both. Brisbane Live Arena and similar mid-2020s indoor arena projects increasingly specify hybrid systems. The duct network is roughly 30 to 40% larger than either pure strategy and drives the most demanding fabrication tolerance specification.

Across all three strategies, bowl supply duct sizing follows 4 to 6 m/s main supply velocity, 2.5 to 3.5 m/s branch velocity, and is oversized relative to commercial-office practice because stadium bowl noise targets are NC-30 or tighter. The supply ducts are always larger than first-pass commercial sizing would suggest, and the structural impact on the bowl edge architecture must be coordinated from concept design.

Premium suite HVAC — the highest acoustic standard in the venue

Premium suites are the acoustic and revenue-density frontier of modern stadiums. A 60,000-seat venue with 80 to 120 premium suites can generate 30 to 50% of total venue revenue from approximately 5 to 8% of the seat count. The HVAC specification reflects this — every premium suite gets a dedicated AHU (or one AHU per cluster of 4 to 6 suites for tighter projects), independent occupant control, and an acoustic specification two to three full NC bands tighter than the general bowl.

Typical premium suite specification:

  • NC-25 to NC-30 background noise. Achieved with low-velocity terminal supply (under 2.5 m/s at the diffuser face), in-line or duct-mounted sound attenuators on supply and return runs, internally lined ductwork (25 mm fibre-free or coated insulation) for the last 6 metres before the suite, and dedicated suite AHU casings with vibration isolation.
  • VAV terminals. Variable Air Volume terminals with reheat where local heating is required, allowing each suite to control temperature independently.
  • Dedicated AHU per suite or cluster. Eliminates the noise-cross-talk problem of branched VAV systems and gives owners full operational independence.
  • Double-walled casing. Both AHUs and ductwork in the suite envelope use double-wall construction to keep airborne sound transmission below the suite's acoustic separation specification (typically RW 50 to 55 between adjacent suites).
  • Sound attenuators. Both supply and return runs carry packaged splitter or cylindrical attenuators sized for at least 12 to 18 dB attenuation across the 250 Hz to 4 kHz octave bands. Stadium suite attenuator selection is acoustically dominated by the 250 Hz and 500 Hz bands where bowl event noise breaks through.
  • Galvanised G275 with internal acoustic lining. The standard duct material specification. Stainless steel is rarely used in premium suite ductwork itself but appears in the kitchen exhaust pipe runs serving suite-level catering.

For broadcast and media booths, NC-20 is common and demands an even tighter package — double-walled silencers, structural acoustic isolation of the AHU plinth, and dedicated outdoor air intakes positioned to avoid bowl noise breakthrough.

Concourse and circulation HVAC

The concourse is the spectator circulation zone — corridors, retail, food service queue lines, restrooms and access stairs. Concourse HVAC is high-volume, less acoustically demanding than the bowl, but more demanding on smoke management because it is the primary egress route in a fire emergency.

Specification baselines:

  • Demand-controlled ventilation. Concourse occupancy swings from near-zero in the first 90 minutes after gates open to peak density in the last 15 minutes before kick-off, then drops sharply during play, then peaks again at half-time. ASHRAE 62.1 Section 6.2.7 demand-controlled ventilation is essential — fixed ventilation rates burn 40 to 60% more fan energy than DCV-controlled across a typical event-day.
  • NC-45 acoustic target. Concourse spaces tolerate higher background noise than the bowl because crowd noise is the dominant ambient. NC-45 to NC-50 is normal, allowing supply velocities up to 7 m/s and reducing duct sizing relative to bowl supply.
  • Smoke purge integration. Concourse supply ducts double as smoke exhaust during a fire emergency in many designs. Sequencing logic must reverse fan direction or open dampered crossover paths between supply and exhaust on smoke detection. NFPA 92 integrity requirements drive duct material selection here — typically 1.0 to 1.6 mm galvanised G275 with rated penetration sealing at compartment boundaries.
  • Fabric duct. Concourse atriums and large open volumes are increasingly served by fabric duct systems (Durkeesox, Prihoda and similar) hung from the structural ceiling. Fabric duct delivers low-velocity supply with minimal visual impact and is acoustically benign but requires specialist supply and installation outside the standard sheet-metal scope.

The concourse duct network is typically the largest by total m² (often 35 to 45% of stadium duct package) but the lowest on a per-m² fabrication cost because tolerances are looser and acoustic lining requirements are reduced.

Locker room and player facility HVAC

Locker rooms are the most demanding humidity-control zone in any stadium. Shower-cycle peaks drive instantaneous moisture release rates 8 to 12 times the normalised baseline, and the recovery window between matches in a back-to-back fixture programme can be as tight as 24 hours.

Specification baselines:

  • Dedicated dehumidification. Locker room HVAC almost never relies on cooling-coil dehumidification alone. Dedicated desiccant or refrigerant-cycle dehumidification units with rapid pull-down are standard, sized to recover from 95% relative humidity to design 50% within 60 to 90 minutes post-match.
  • Exhaust at 2.5 to 5 L/s/m². ASHRAE 62.1 Table 6-4 minimum exhaust rates for locker rooms and shower areas. AS 1668.2 Section 3 carries similar values. Exhaust ducts must be moisture-tolerant — typically 304L stainless steel in the shower-room directly served runs, transitioning to galvanised G275 downstream of the shower-room compartment boundary.
  • Pressure regime. Locker rooms are net-negative with respect to adjacent corridors and player lounges to prevent moisture and odour migration. A 5 to 10 Pa negative pressure is typical and drives the supply-versus-exhaust airflow balance during commissioning.
  • Player lounge and warm-up area integration. The locker room HVAC zone usually extends to the player warm-up corridor, medical room and kit-storage room. Each subzone has its own thermal and humidity targets but shares the dehumidification capacity with the locker room itself.
  • Material specification. 304L stainless steel duct in shower areas (not 316 — the chloride exposure is from showering, not seawater, and 304L is sufficient at half the material cost). Galvanised G275 elsewhere.

Catering kitchen HVAC — NFPA 96 governance

Stadium catering kitchens combine the highest grease loads in the venue with the longest exhaust runs because kitchens are usually located in BOH zones with riser routes climbing through multiple compartment boundaries to reach roof discharge. NFPA 96 governs the entire chain.

Specification fundamentals:

  • Type I hoods over cooking equipment. Required over any equipment producing grease-laden vapour — fryers, charbroilers, ranges, woks, salamanders. Type I hoods discharge into grease ducts.
  • Type II hoods over heat or steam producing equipment. Dishwashers, steam kettles, hot food holding. Type II discharge runs in galvanised duct, no grease-rated requirement.
  • Grease ducts. NFPA 96 Section 7 requires liquid-tight continuous external welded construction. Minimum thickness 1.4 mm (16 gauge) carbon steel or 1.1 mm (18 gauge) stainless steel. Stadium projects routinely specify Schedule 10 black steel (3.4 mm wall) for the riser sections to allow welded brackets and longer service life. No flange-and-gasket or screwed connections permitted on grease ducts — only continuous external welds with leak-tested seams.
  • Fire-rated enclosure or factory-listed assembly. Grease ducts must be either enclosed in a 2-hour fire-rated shaft or factory-listed as a self-rated assembly per UL 1978. Stadium designs typically use shaft enclosure for risers and listed assembly for the horizontal runs in the kitchen ceiling void.
  • Cleanout access spacing. NFPA 96 requires cleanout access at every change of direction and at maximum 4.0 m intervals on horizontal runs. Stadium grease-duct routing through complex BOH ceiling voids drives the access-panel count high — 80 to 140 cleanout panels are typical on a 60,000-seat venue.
  • Replacement air. NFPA 96 requires balanced replacement air for kitchen exhaust — typically 80 to 90% of the exhaust airflow. Replacement air units must be interlocked with hood operation through the BMS so replacement air starts before exhaust and stops after.
  • Automatic fire suppression integration. Type I hoods carry UL 300 listed wet-chemical suppression systems. The suppression activation must shut down cooking fuel supply and exhaust fan motor automatically.
  • Welded fabrication. The continuous external welds required by NFPA 96 demand a duct welding machine with full traceability — welds must be auditable for inspection. SBKJ duct welding machines deliver this with documented welder qualification records and weld procedure specifications per AWS D1.1.

Aquatic facility HVAC — pool dehumidification within stadium envelope

Several modern stadium projects co-locate a training pool, hydrotherapy facility or full Olympic aquatic centre within the broader stadium envelope. Brisbane Aquatic Centre is a stand-alone Olympic-grade example, but smaller training pools embedded inside stadium athletic facilities are increasingly common.

Pool HVAC differs fundamentally from spectator HVAC:

  • Continuous dehumidification. Pool halls run 24/7 dehumidification because evaporation is continuous. Spectator and sports HVAC, by contrast, peaks during events. The pool AHU package is typically a packaged dehumidifier with refrigerant-cycle desiccant or heat-pump-recovery dehumidification.
  • Chlorine corrosion. Pool hall atmospheres carry trace chloramine and chlorine that corrode standard galvanised duct rapidly. 304L stainless steel is the minimum specification for pool hall supply and exhaust ducts. 316L is sometimes specified for ducts directly above the pool surface.
  • Pool-deck supply. Pool decks are heated and dehumidified through perimeter slot diffusers or under-window plenums to manage condensation on glazed pool envelopes. The supply duct sizing is driven by dehumidification airflow rather than sensible cooling.
  • Spectator zone separation. Pool spectator stands have their own ventilation zone — typically less aggressive than the pool hall itself but still requiring dehumidification. Brisbane Aquatic Centre and similar Olympic projects specify two distinct zones with crossover dampers.

Retractable roof HVAC challenges

Retractable-roof venues are the most demanding HVAC operating regime in stadium design. The HVAC system must run two distinct modes interlocked through the BMS to the roof position sensor.

Roof-open mode. The bowl operates with natural ventilation. Bowl AHUs are throttled to perimeter dehumidification only. Premium suites and concourse continue full mechanical operation. Smoke management remains active. The fan-power saving relative to roof-closed mode is substantial — typically 60 to 75% reduction in bowl AHU energy.

Roof-closed mode. Bowl AHUs ramp to full output, typically 40 to 60 air changes per hour for 60,000 to 90,000 spectators. The roof closure event itself triggers a ramp sequence on the BMS — full output within 10 to 15 minutes of full-closed signal. The roof position sensor is safety-critical and must fail to closed-mode HVAC operation if signal is lost.

Transition mode. The 5 to 10 minute window during which the roof is partially open or closed is a transition state. Modern BMS sequencing maintains roof-closed-mode HVAC throughout the transition rather than ramping in lockstep with the roof — air-handling response time is slower than mechanical roof movement, so pre-emptive operation is more efficient.

Suncorp Stadium, Marvel Stadium and the planned Gabba retractable roof all carry this dual-mode HVAC sequencing. Specification engineers must coordinate the roof control vendor's signal output with the BMS HVAC sequencing controller — a single common-mode failure on the position sensor cascades into HVAC operating-mode error and is the highest-risk failure mode in retractable-roof venue commissioning.

Smoke management — NFPA 92 and IBC 909

Stadium smoke management is governed by NFPA 92 in NFPA jurisdictions and by IBC Chapter 909 in IBC jurisdictions. Both codes recognise the assembly-occupancy challenge: 60,000 to 100,000 occupants needing safe egress from a single envelope means smoke exhaust capacity must be substantial.

Three distinct smoke management systems typically operate in a stadium:

  1. Bowl smoke purge. The bowl supply fans reverse direction or open crossover dampers on smoke detection, exhausting the bowl at high volume to maintain visibility above 1.8 m floor level. Fan capacity for bowl smoke purge is sized per NFPA 92 Section 5 with the smoke layer interface height as the primary design parameter.
  2. Atrium smoke control. Concourse atriums and grand concourse spaces above 17 m height are governed by NFPA 92 Section 5.3. Smoke exhaust from the atrium ceiling, makeup air at the atrium floor, and pressurisation differential maintained between atrium and adjacent egress.
  3. Stairwell pressurisation. Egress stairs are pressurised positive relative to adjacent floors per NFPA 92 Section 4.6. The pressurisation fans, supply duct and stair-leak path sizing are coordinated as a system.

Smoke management duct integrity must be tested per UL 555 (fire dampers), UL 555S (smoke dampers), UL 1979 (smoke control duct) or AS 1530.4 in Australian jurisdictions. Stadium specification packages typically require 60-minute integrity and stability for ducts crossing fire compartments, 120-minute for ducts in egress shafts. The duct material is normally galvanised G275 in 1.0 to 1.6 mm thickness with TDF flange connection and rated penetration sealing at compartment boundaries — the SBKJ TDF flange former produces this exact specification with consistent mechanical strength under fire-test conditions.

Event-day versus idle-mode operation

The 80 to 90% load swing between event-day and idle-day is the dominant economic challenge in stadium HVAC operation. Specifying the duct system right means specifying not just for peak event load but for the operating regime spanning idle to peak.

Sequencing logic typically defines four distinct modes:

  • Idle mode. No event scheduled. Bowl AHUs off, premium suite AHUs in setback, concourse minimum ventilation. Locker room and BOH on baseline schedules. Total fan power 5 to 12% of peak event mode.
  • Pre-event mode. Triggered 4 to 6 hours before scheduled event start. Bowl AHUs ramp to setpoint, premium suites pre-cool, concourse ventilation ramps to ASHRAE 62.1 occupied minimum. The pre-event ramp is sized to achieve setpoint by gates-open time.
  • Event mode. Full peak operation. Bowl, suites, concourse, locker room, kitchen and BOH all at design output. Demand-controlled ventilation modulates concourse and select suite zones based on actual occupancy.
  • Post-event mode. Triggered at gates-closed signal. Bowl AHUs ramp down to setback over 60 to 90 minutes, suites return to idle setback, concourse minimum ventilation, locker room continues high dehumidification through to the post-match shower cycle complete.

Each mode has explicit BMS sequencing logic. Stadium commissioning engineers must run each mode through a documented test sequence at handover, with first-article event-mode operation proven against a simulated occupancy load.

Material specifications by zone

The stadium duct network is not a single material specification — it is six or seven distinct material zones bundled into a single fabrication and installation package. Getting the material specification right by zone is the single largest cost-control lever in the duct package.

  • Bowl rectangular supply. Galvanised G275 (Z275) coated steel, 0.8 to 1.6 mm wall thickness depending on duct cross-section. Internally lined with 25 to 50 mm acoustic insulation for the last 6 metres before bowl diffusers. Class A leakage per AS/NZS 4254. SBKJ SBAL-V auto duct line is specifically sized for this scope at high-volume production rates.
  • Concourse atrium fabric duct. Polyester or fluoropolymer fabric duct from specialist suppliers (Durkeesox, Prihoda). Hung from structural ceiling on tensioned cable systems. Outside the standard sheet-metal scope but coordinated with the rigid backbone.
  • Round duct in atrium and concourse. Spiral seam-locked round duct in 0.6 to 1.2 mm galvanised G275, fabricated on a spiral tubeformer. Used where round ductwork is specified by architectural intent or where round geometry reduces pressure loss in long runs. SBKJ SBTF spiral tubeformer covers this scope.
  • Premium suite ductwork. Galvanised G275 with double-wall casing and internal acoustic lining. TDF flange for the heavier sections, slip-and-drive for smaller branches. Fabricated to AS/NZS 4254 Class A.
  • Locker room shower zone. 304L stainless steel duct, 0.8 to 1.2 mm wall thickness. Continuous external welded construction in the shower-room directly served runs. Galvanised G275 downstream of compartment boundary.
  • Kitchen Type I grease duct. Schedule 10 black carbon steel (3.4 mm wall) or minimum 1.4 mm carbon steel per NFPA 96. Continuous external welded construction. Fire-rated enclosure or factory-listed assembly. SBKJ duct welding machines fabricate this scope to AWS D1.1.
  • Kitchen Type II hood exhaust. Galvanised G275 in 1.0 to 1.6 mm wall thickness. Standard sheet metal construction.
  • Smoke management duct. Galvanised G275 in 1.0 to 1.6 mm wall thickness, TDF flange connection, rated penetration sealing. Tested to UL 1979 or AS 1530.4 for integrity and stability. Fabricated on the SBKJ SBAL-V auto duct line with TDF flange former integration.
  • Pool hall duct. 304L stainless steel minimum, 316L for ducts directly above pool surface. Continuous external welded construction in critical pool-deck runs. Drainage falls coordinated to evacuate condensation.

Acoustic specifications

Stadium acoustic specification by zone, from quietest to loudest:

  • NC-20 — broadcast and media booth. Most demanding zone. Double-walled silencers, structural acoustic isolation of AHU plinth, dedicated outdoor air intake away from bowl.
  • NC-25 — premium suite, executive lounge, dressing room. Low-velocity terminal supply, in-line attenuators, double-wall casing, internally lined ductwork.
  • NC-30 — general bowl spectator zone, members' lounge. Standard bowl specification. Internally lined supply duct for last 6 m before diffuser, supply velocity 4 to 6 m/s.
  • NC-35 — concourse retail, food and beverage seating. Tighter than general concourse because patrons are stationary.
  • NC-40 — concourse restroom, BOH support spaces. Standard commercial-grade specification.
  • NC-45 — concourse circulation, primary spectator pathway. Standard concourse specification. Allows higher supply velocities and smaller duct cross-sections than the bowl.
  • NC-50 — service corridor, plant room access, kitchen prep. Functional spaces only. Standard sheet-metal duct construction with minimal acoustic treatment.

The acoustic specification drives both duct sizing (lower NC means lower velocity means larger duct) and acoustic treatment (sound attenuators, internal lining, double-wall casing). On a typical stadium project, the acoustic treatment scope adds 15 to 25% to the base duct package cost — value-engineered specifications that drop one NC band per zone can save substantial capital while breaking the original acoustic design intent.

Construction phasing — shell-and-core then fitout

Stadium HVAC is rarely constructed in a single continuous campaign. The standard phasing model is:

  1. Shell-and-core phase. Bowl supply mains, concourse riser duct, smoke management shafts, plant-room AHU farms, and primary BOH risers are installed during the structural and envelope phase. The duct package at this phase is roughly 55 to 65% of total scope and is fabricated on the standard SBKJ SBAL-V auto duct line at high production rates.
  2. Fitout phase. Premium suite ductwork, locker room branch runs, kitchen exhaust, retail tenancies and final concourse branches install during the fitout phase. The remaining 35 to 45% of the duct package is fabricated to project-specific specifications with tighter tolerance and acoustic treatment.
  3. Commissioning phase. Air balance, leakage testing, smoke purge functional test, BMS sequencing test, and event-mode load simulation. Commissioning typically runs 2 to 3 months on a major stadium project.

Separate AHU farms for premium versus general are standard. Premium suite AHUs cluster in dedicated plant rooms with vibration isolation, sound attenuation, and independent BMS zones. General bowl and concourse AHUs cluster in larger plant rooms typically located at sub-stand or under-bowl level for gravity duct routing into the bowl envelope.

Specification procedure — 20 steps from concept to commissioning

The specification procedure below is the same one SBKJ engineers walk through with mechanical contractors and consultants on stadium projects. Each step is checked off before progressing to the next — skipping steps correlates strongly with rework on commissioning day.

  1. Confirm venue type and occupancy. Classify the venue as outdoor open stadium, retractable roof, fully enclosed indoor arena, multi-purpose venue or training facility.
  2. Establish design occupancy by zone. Define peak occupancy for bowl, concourse, premium suite, locker room, BOH, broadcast, retail and catering separately.
  3. Apply ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation rates. Use Table 6-1 occupancy classification with breathing-zone outdoor air rate Vbz computed for each zone.
  4. Cross-check AS 1668.2 for Australian venues. Run a parallel calculation under AS 1668.2 Section 3 — sometimes more stringent than ASHRAE on assembly occupancy.
  5. Define event-mode versus idle-mode loads. Map the 80 to 90% load swing across both modes and sequence the AHU staging logic before duct sizing.
  6. Select bowl ventilation strategy. Choose perimeter slot diffuser, under-seat displacement, or hybrid bowl supply. Drives duct routing, fascia plenum size and diffuser specification.
  7. Size bowl supply ducts. Apply 4 to 6 m/s main supply velocity, 2.5 to 3.5 m/s branch velocity. Bowl ducts are oversized to keep noise under NC-30 in occupied zones.
  8. Specify premium suite AHUs. One dedicated AHU per suite (or per cluster of 4 to 6 suites), NC-25 to NC-30 background noise, double-wall casing, sound attenuators on supply and return.
  9. Design concourse high-volume air handling. Demand-controlled ventilation per ASHRAE 62.1, NC-45 acoustic target, integration with bowl smoke purge per NFPA 92.
  10. Specify locker room HVAC. Dedicated dehumidification, rapid recovery between matches, exhaust at 2.5 to 5 L/s/m², 304L stainless duct in shower zones.
  11. Design kitchen exhaust per NFPA 96. Type I hoods over cooking equipment, Type II over dishwashing, grease ducts continuous welded black steel Schedule 10 with fire-rated enclosure.
  12. Specify smoke management per NFPA 92. Bowl smoke purge, atrium smoke control, exhaust shaft sizing per IBC 909. Duct integrity per UL 555 or AS 1530.4.
  13. Interlock retractable roof to HVAC mode. Roof-open mode natural ventilation only, roof-closed mode full mechanical, fail-safe to closed-mode HVAC.
  14. Define BMS sequencing logic. Event-mode versus idle-mode staging, demand-controlled ventilation triggers, smoke purge override, kitchen makeup air interlock.
  15. Specify acoustic treatment. Internally lined duct (25 to 50 mm) on last 6 m before bowl and suite diffusers, sound attenuators on AHU discharge.
  16. Plan construction phasing. Shell-and-core delivers main bowl and concourse, fitout phase delivers suite AHUs and BOH branches.
  17. Select duct material by zone. Galvanised G275 for bowl, fabric duct for concourse atrium, 304L stainless for locker room and kitchen Type II, black steel Schedule 10 welded for grease.
  18. Specify fabrication tolerances. AS/NZS 4254 Class A for bowl, Class B for BOH, ±2 mm cross-section, leakage Class A or B per EN 12237.
  19. Commission with on-site testing. Air balance per AABC or NEBB, leakage per EN 12237 or SMACNA, smoke purge functional test, full event-mode load simulation.
  20. Prepare for legacy and major-event modes. Olympic and FIFA-grade venues require dual-mode commissioning — Games-mode and legacy-mode tested separately.

Common specification mistakes

The five mistakes below account for the majority of stadium HVAC commissioning failures and rework.

  1. Specifying bowl ducts at commercial-office velocity. Bowl supply at 8 to 10 m/s passes desk-check but breaks NC-30 acoustic targets and triggers full duct re-sizing during commissioning. Always size at 4 to 6 m/s main, 2.5 to 3.5 m/s branch.
  2. Treating retractable-roof venues as single-mode. Designing only for roof-closed peak load misses the energy waste in roof-open operation. Specify dual-mode BMS sequencing from concept and interlock to a roof position sensor with documented fail-safe behaviour.
  3. Underspecifying premium suite acoustic treatment. Suites delivered at NC-35 (matching general bowl) lose premium-revenue defensibility. Always specify NC-25 to NC-30 with full attenuator package and double-wall AHU casing.
  4. Cutting kitchen grease duct corners on welded construction. NFPA 96 is unambiguous — continuous external welded only. Flange-and-gasket grease duct fails inspection, loses occupancy permit, and triggers full re-fabrication.
  5. Skipping smoke management functional testing. Bowl smoke purge sequencing and atrium smoke control must be functionally tested under live BMS sequencing before handover. Paper-based commissioning sign-off without functional smoke purge test correlates strongly with post-occupancy fire-marshal inspection failures.

FAQ

What is the typical lead time for stadium-grade HVAC ductwork on a major sports venue project?

Stadium HVAC duct fabrication for a 60,000-seat venue typically requires 14 to 22 months end-to-end — 3 to 4 months for design and shop-drawing approval, 2 to 3 months for machinery commissioning if a dedicated fabrication line is set up, 9 to 14 months for fabrication and progressive site delivery, and 1 to 2 months for commissioning and balancing. Olympic and FIFA World Cup venues compress this to 18 months by running parallel fabrication lines for bowl, concourse, premium suite and BOH simultaneously.

What fire-rating is required for stadium ductwork?

Stadium ductwork fire-rating depends on the zone. Bowl smoke-purge ducts crossing fire compartments require 60-minute integrity and stability tested to AS 1530.4 or EN 1366-1. Kitchen grease ducts require 2-hour rated enclosure or factory-built listed grease duct per NFPA 96. Smoke management ducts in atrium and concourse spaces typically require 60 to 120 minutes per NFPA 92 and IBC 909. Locker room and BOH ducts crossing rated assemblies require fire dampers or combination fire-smoke dampers listed to UL 555 and UL 555S.

What acoustic specification is typical for stadium premium suites?

Premium suites in stadiums and arenas typically specify NC-25 to NC-30 background noise from HVAC. Achieved with low-velocity terminal supply (under 2.5 m/s at the diffuser face), in-line or duct-mounted sound attenuators on supply and return, internally lined ductwork (25 mm fibre-free or coated insulation) for the last 6 metres before the suite, and dedicated suite AHUs with isolation hangers. Broadcast and media booths typically specify NC-20 with double-walled silencers plus structural acoustic isolation of the AHU plinth.

How does HVAC operate in a stadium with a retractable roof?

Retractable-roof stadiums run two distinct HVAC modes interlocked through the BMS to the roof position sensor. Roof-open mode operates the bowl with natural ventilation supplemented by perimeter dehumidification only. Roof-closed mode activates full bowl AHU output, typically 40 to 60 air changes per hour for 60,000 to 90,000 spectators. The roof position interlock is safety-critical and must fail to closed-mode HVAC operation.

What HVAC scope changes for Olympic Brisbane 2032 venues?

Olympic Brisbane 2032 venues are designed to two specifications: Games-mode and legacy-mode. Games-mode HVAC includes broadcast compound cooling, anti-doping suite isolation, athlete medical and warm-up zones, expanded media work-room ventilation and accessible spectator zone supply uprated for 100% ticket sale density. Legacy-mode reverts to AFL or NRL operating density (typically 60 to 75% of Games density). The Gabba redevelopment and Brisbane Live Arena are both being designed dual-mode from concept stage.

What duct material is specified for stadium kitchen grease exhaust?

NFPA 96 requires commercial kitchen grease exhaust ducts to be liquid-tight continuously welded black carbon steel of minimum 1.4 mm thickness, or stainless steel of minimum 1.1 mm. Stadium catering kitchens routinely specify Schedule 10 black steel (3.4 mm) for the riser sections to allow welded brackets and longer service life. Flange-and-gasket connections are not permitted on grease ducts — only continuous external welds with leak-tested seams.

How is bowl ventilation typically distributed across stadium seating?

Three strategies dominate. (1) Perimeter slot-diffuser supply throws conditioned air down the seating tier from edge-of-roof or fascia plenum runs. (2) Under-seat displacement supply delivers low-velocity air at floor level rising through occupants. (3) Hybrid bowl supply combines both, used in fully enclosed indoor arenas. All three require oversized supply ducts because stadium peak loads are sensible-cooling dominated.

What machinery is required to fabricate stadium HVAC ductwork at scale?

A 60,000-seat stadium typically requires 80,000 to 140,000 m² of ductwork fabricated over 14 to 22 months. Standard machinery loadout is two SBAL-V auto duct lines for galvanised rectangular bowl and concourse supply, one SBTF spiral tubeformer for round atrium and concourse runs, one TDF flange former for tight smoke management duct in service shafts, and one duct welding machine for kitchen grease and bowl smoke purge duct.
