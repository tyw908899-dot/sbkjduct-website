1. United States market size and structure

The US HVAC ductwork installation market is conservatively in the USD 18–22 billion per year range, depending on whose definitions you use and whether residential rough-in is included. The split is roughly 60–65 percent commercial and institutional construction (offices, healthcare, education, data centres, retail, hospitality and industrial) and 35–40 percent residential (single-family, multifamily and light commercial). Sheet-metal duct — galvanized steel and to a lesser extent stainless — accounts for roughly 75 percent of the rectangular and round duct installed in commercial buildings, with fibrous glass duct board and flexible duct making up most of the remainder.

The fabrication side of the industry — the shops physically converting coil into duct — is highly fragmented. There are several thousand HVAC sheet-metal contractors operating fabrication shops across the country, ranging from single-lockformer regional shops outputting 200–400 metres of duct per shift, through mid-tier shops running one or two auto duct lines at 1,000–2,000 metres per shift, up to the largest national fabricators running multiple auto lines, coil lines, cross-break stations and CNC plasma tables in dedicated 50,000-square-foot facilities. The industry is not consolidated the way air-handler manufacturing or chiller manufacturing is — most fabrication remains within 200–400 miles of the project site because the freight cost of shipping fabricated rectangular duct is high relative to the freight cost of coil.

Geographic distribution of fabrication capacity follows construction activity. The biggest concentrations are the Texas triangle (Dallas-Fort Worth, Houston, San Antonio, Austin), the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast corridor (NYC, Philadelphia, DC), the Southeast (Atlanta, Charlotte, Nashville, Florida), Southern California (LA, Orange County, San Diego), Northern California (Bay Area), the Pacific Northwest (Seattle, Portland), the upper Midwest (Chicago, Minneapolis, Detroit), and the Mountain West (Denver, Phoenix, Salt Lake City, Las Vegas). Each region has its own set of local SMACNA chapter conventions, AHJ preferences and labour-cost realities that shape what equipment makes sense for that market.

Demand drivers in 2025–2027 are dominated by data centre construction (the single fastest-growing vertical for sheet-metal contractors), federal infrastructure projects under the Inflation Reduction Act and CHIPS Act, healthcare and life-sciences buildouts, and the slow but steady electrification retrofit wave hitting commercial buildings as ASHRAE 90.1 tightens and state energy codes pull forward. Residential single-family slowed in 2023–2024 with mortgage rates but is rebounding. Multifamily remains a steady contributor.

2. SMACNA HVAC Duct Construction Standards — 4th Edition

The SMACNA HVAC Duct Construction Standards — Metal and Flexible, 4th Edition (published by the Sheet Metal and Air Conditioning Contractors' National Association) is the single most-cited fabrication standard in US construction specifications. If your shop fabricates rectangular or round metal duct for any project that will be inspected, the SMACNA 4th Edition is on the project's reference standards list. The document defines pressure classes, seal classes, gauge tables, reinforcement member spacing and joint construction in a single coherent system.

Pressure classes

SMACNA defines duct pressure classes by the design static pressure the duct must withstand without exceeding deflection limits. The classes published in the 4th Edition are 0.5 in. wg, 1 in. wg, 2 in. wg, 3 in. wg, 4 in. wg, 6 in. wg, and 10 in. wg, in both positive and negative pressure variants where relevant. In commercial practice the dominant classes are:

  • 0.5 in. wg — return air ductwork in many low-pressure systems, residential and light-commercial supply.
  • 1 in. wg — common return-air spec on commercial systems.
  • 2 in. wg — the workhorse class for commercial supply duct downstream of the air handler. The vast majority of office, retail, education and hospitality duct is specified at 2 in. wg positive.
  • 3 in. wg — higher-pressure commercial supply, larger floor plates, longer duct runs.
  • 4 in. wg — data centres, healthcare, laboratory and industrial systems with longer runs or higher external static.
  • 6 in. wg / 10 in. wg — high-pressure industrial process supply, fume exhaust on chemical and semiconductor plants.

Pressure class drives gauge selection. A 2 in. wg duct with a 36-inch wide side at 24 ga is acceptable; the same duct at 4 in. wg requires either a heavier gauge (22 or 20 ga) or tighter reinforcement spacing. Specifying duct above the actual operating pressure costs money in coil and labour for no functional benefit. Specifying below it fails the leakage test or, worse, deflects in service.

Seal classes A, B and C

SMACNA seal class defines what surfaces of the duct must be sealed to limit air leakage. The three classes in current practice are:

  • Seal Class A — all transverse joints, longitudinal seams, and duct wall penetrations sealed. This is the most stringent and is now effectively the floor for US commercial work because ASHRAE 90.1 leakage limits drive the project specification toward class A.
  • Seal Class B — all transverse joints and longitudinal seams sealed, but duct wall penetrations not necessarily sealed. Legacy specification.
  • Seal Class C — only transverse joints sealed. Legacy specification on lower-pressure return systems.

Specifying for seal class A means your TDF and Pittsburgh tooling must produce a joint geometry that accepts the project's chosen sealant — typically a UL 181-listed water-based mastic or a UL 181B-listed pressure-sensitive tape — and that the FAT acceptance criteria explicitly include leakage testing on a representative duct section. If your shop is already running TDF flanges and Pittsburgh longitudinal seams with pre-applied gasket, you are already configured for class A.

Gauge tables and reinforcement

The SMACNA gauge tables are the bread and butter of the standard. Gauge selection for a given duct depends on (a) the duct's largest cross-sectional dimension, (b) the design pressure class, and (c) the reinforcement spacing chosen. As duct dimension increases or pressure class increases, the gauge thickens. As reinforcement spacing tightens, a thinner gauge becomes acceptable.

The most commonly used US sheet-metal gauges in HVAC duct fabrication are:

  • 26 ga — 0.0179 in. (0.45 mm), light residential and small commercial low-pressure return.
  • 24 ga — 0.0276 in. (0.70 mm), the dominant commercial low-pressure gauge. Most 2 in. wg supply duct under 30-inch wide is 24 ga.
  • 22 ga — 0.0336 in. (0.85 mm), 2 in. wg duct above 30-inch wide, or 4 in. wg duct under 30-inch wide.
  • 20 ga — 0.0396 in. (1.00 mm), heavier reinforcement-zone duct, larger 4 in. wg duct.
  • 18 ga — 0.0516 in. (1.31 mm), heavy industrial supply, large data centre supply duct.
  • 16 ga — 0.0598 in. (1.52 mm), industrial process exhaust, structural duct supports, elbow throats on heavy ducts.

Reinforcement is typically by external angles, channels or hat sections at intervals defined by the SMACNA tables. Auto duct lines such as the SBKJ SBAL-V series integrate cross-break and TDF flanging in-line so the longitudinal seam, transverse joint and reinforcement plane are produced in one pass at production rate.

3. Key US codes and standards beyond SMACNA

SMACNA defines how the duct is built. The codes below define what the building department, fire marshal and energy auditor will hold you to. Every active US duct shop should have working knowledge of all of them.

International Mechanical Code (IMC)

The IMC, published by the International Code Council, is the dominant mechanical code adopted in some form by most US states and local jurisdictions. The IMC chapter on ducts (Chapter 6 in the 2018, 2021 and 2024 editions) references SMACNA for sheet-metal duct construction, sets minimum air-quality and insulation requirements, governs material flame-spread and smoke-developed indices, and dictates installation requirements such as duct support spacing and clearances to combustibles. Most state amendments to the IMC are in the residential mechanical code or in fire protection appendices rather than in core duct construction.

NFPA 90A and 90B

NFPA 90A — Standard for the Installation of Air-Conditioning and Ventilating Systems — and NFPA 90B — Standard for the Installation of Warm Air Heating and Air-Conditioning Systems — are the National Fire Protection Association documents governing the fire-protection side of HVAC. They cover combustible insulation limits, fire dampers, smoke dampers, smoke detectors in air handlers, return-air plenum construction and the restrictions on ducts passing through fire-rated assemblies. NFPA 90A applies to commercial systems; 90B to residential and light-commercial. Most US AHJs reference NFPA 90A by default for any system above a small residential split.

ASHRAE 90.1-2022

ASHRAE Standard 90.1 — Energy Standard for Sites and Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential Buildings — is the dominant commercial energy standard. The 2022 edition tightens duct leakage requirements (now expressed as a maximum cfm/100 ft² of duct surface at design pressure), specifies minimum duct insulation R-values by climate zone, and prescribes commissioning requirements. ASHRAE 90.1 is referenced by IECC, by federal procurement requirements (GSA, ESPC, Buy Clean) and by many state energy codes. If you fabricate for any project pursuing LEED, WELL, ENERGY STAR or federal compliance, ASHRAE 90.1-2022 leakage limits are likely binding on your duct.

IECC — International Energy Conservation Code

The IECC is the residential and commercial energy code adopted in most states. The commercial provisions reference ASHRAE 90.1 directly. The residential provisions specify duct leakage limits per Section R403, duct insulation R-values per climate zone, and rough-in air leakage testing requirements. For residential and light-commercial duct fabricators, IECC governs more day-to-day work than ASHRAE 90.1.

UL 181, 181A and 181B

UL 181 — Standard for Factory-Made Air Ducts and Connectors — and its companion UL 181A (closure systems for rigid duct) and UL 181B (closure systems for flexible duct) cover the listings that duct sealants, mastics and tapes must carry. When a project specifies seal class A, the project specification almost always also specifies UL 181-listed sealant. When you run the FAT on a duct line, the seal is part of the demonstration — even though the sealant is field-applied, the joint geometry must accept the listed sealant without re-engineering.

SMACNA companion documents

Beyond the core HVAC Duct Construction Standards, SMACNA publishes several companion documents your shop should have on the bookshelf: SMACNA Round Industrial Duct Construction Standards, SMACNA Rectangular Industrial Duct Construction Standards, SMACNA HVAC Air Duct Leakage Test Manual (which defines the field leakage test referenced by ASHRAE 90.1), SMACNA HVAC Systems Duct Design, and SMACNA Seismic Restraint Manual. The Leakage Test Manual is particularly important because it defines the leakage classes and field-test method most US commissioning agents follow.

4. Regional code overlays and state-specific requirements

The IMC plus referenced standards is the floor; states and major cities pile on amendments. Five overlays are large enough to materially affect how a duct shop fabricates and what it ships into a region.

California — Title 24

California Title 24, Part 6 — Building Energy Efficiency Standards — is the most demanding state energy code in the US. For HVAC duct fabrication the relevant provisions are tighter duct leakage limits than IECC default, mandatory duct sealing tests on residential systems, mandatory commissioning on commercial systems above defined thresholds, and an evolving set of all-electric and embodied carbon requirements. Title 24 is updated on a three-year cycle (2019, 2022, 2025 editions). California also has its own seismic anchorage requirements that affect duct support design more than fabrication, but a fabricator shipping into California should expect every project specification to reference both SMACNA and California-specific seismic provisions.

Florida — High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ)

Miami-Dade and Broward Counties operate under the Florida Building Code with the High Velocity Hurricane Zone overlay, which requires Notice of Acceptance (NOA) approvals for many building products, mandates wind-load calculations on rooftop equipment and outdoor duct, and tightens fastener and support specifications. For duct fabrication specifically, HVHZ mostly affects exterior duct and the equipment supports rather than interior duct, but a fabricator quoting on Miami high-rise work should expect NOA documentation requirements on any product that crosses the building envelope.

New York City

The New York City Mechanical Code is a heavily amended version of the IMC, with additional provisions on smoke control, plenum construction in high-rise buildings, and mandatory sprinklering requirements that affect duct routing. The NYC Department of Buildings approves licensed master plumbers and licensed master fire suppression contractors, and on large commercial projects a Site Safety Plan is required. None of this changes the fabrication itself materially — a 2 in. wg 24 ga duct with TDF flanges fabricated to SMACNA is still acceptable in NYC — but the documentation burden and the AHJ review timeline are significantly heavier than in most US jurisdictions.

Texas — energy code and IMC

Texas adopts the IECC and IMC at state level with relatively light amendments. The Texas market is dominated by Dallas-Fort Worth and Houston, both of which run their own building department interpretations but generally follow the IMC and SMACNA closely. Texas is one of the largest single-state markets for sheet-metal duct fabrication and one of the easiest states to operate a duct shop in from a code perspective — most of the project-specific complexity comes from owner specs (data centre owners, healthcare systems) rather than from the city or state code.

Pacific Northwest — Washington and Oregon

Washington State Energy Code and Oregon Energy Efficiency Specialty Code both run ahead of IECC default in several areas, with stricter duct leakage and insulation requirements. Seattle has its own stretch code with heat-pump and electrification requirements. Both states reference SMACNA for fabrication; the energy provisions land on the design and commissioning side rather than on coil-to-duct fabrication itself.

5. The US duct fabrication industry — players and structure

The US HVAC sheet-metal industry is structured into three loosely overlapping tiers, and which tier you target determines what equipment you should buy.

Tier 1 — National sheet-metal contractors and large regional fabricators. These are the shops attached to the largest mechanical contractors, often unionised under SMART (the Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers union), running multiple auto duct lines, coil lines, plasma tables and cross-break stations in dedicated 30,000–80,000 square foot facilities. They serve hospital systems, data centre developers, federal buildings, university campuses and large commercial general contractors. Output is typically 5,000–15,000 metres of duct per shift across multiple lines. Equipment is updated on 12–18 year refresh cycles.

Tier 2 — Mid-tier regional fabricators. Independent sheet-metal contractors running one or two auto duct lines, a Pittsburgh lockformer, TDF flanger and ancillary equipment in 8,000–25,000 square foot facilities. Output is 800–3,000 metres of duct per shift. They serve mid-market commercial general contractors, regional mechanical contractors, smaller data centre and healthcare projects, and their own internal install crews. Most US sheet-metal contractors fall in this tier, and this tier is the largest single buyer of new auto duct lines and spiral tubeformers.

Tier 3 — Small shops and specialty fabricators. Single-lockformer shops, custom fabricators, industrial duct specialists and shops attached to small mechanical contractors. Equipment is often a Pittsburgh lockformer plus a single power notcher, a 4–6 foot box-and-pan brake, and a stomper for TDF. Output is 200–500 metres per shift, often hand-rolled or Pittsburgh-only. The transition from tier 3 to tier 2 — the "buy your first auto duct line" decision — is the single biggest equipment-purchase milestone in the US duct fabrication industry, and it is the conversation we have most often with US customers.

Outside these tiers there is a smaller specialty layer of industrial process duct fabricators (heavy-gauge welded duct for power plants, refineries, pulp and paper, semiconductor fume exhaust) and a residential-only layer of HVAC contractors who fabricate locally for their own jobs. Both segments exist; the SBKJ machine portfolio touches both via the spiral tubeformer for round industrial duct and the smaller-format auto duct lines for residential and light-commercial.

6. Equipment specification for the US market

A machine designed for the European or Asia-Pacific market and shipped to the US without re-specification will fail the AHJ inspection, void the warranty if rewired in the field, and cost the buyer 10–15 percent of machine value in retrofit. The five specification points below are the difference between a machine that drops onto a US shop floor and starts producing, and a machine that becomes a six-month commissioning project.

480V 60Hz three-phase power

Standard US industrial three-phase power is 480V 60Hz. Auxiliary single-phase loads run at 208V or 240V. The European standard of 380V/400V 50Hz and the Asia-Pacific 380V 50Hz are not interchangeable with 480V 60Hz — motors run at the wrong speed and torque, drives miscalculate, transformers overheat, and the machine specification is no longer what you ordered.

SBKJ ships machines configured for 480V 60Hz to North American customers as standard. That means motors are sized and wound for 60Hz, variable frequency drives are 480V-rated and tuned for 60Hz output, control transformers step 480V down to the required control voltage (120V or 24V DC depending on circuit), and the PLC power supply runs from a stable 60Hz source. Customers in the lower 48 states, Hawaii and Alaska all receive the same 480V 60Hz package.

UL 508A control panels

UL 508A — the Standard for Industrial Control Panels — is the listing US AHJs expect to see on the control cabinet of any machine plugged into a US industrial electrical service. UL 508A is not a national legal requirement, but in practice every state and local AHJ we have worked with treats UL 508A as the floor. The label is built by an accredited industrial control panel shop that meets UL 508A construction requirements: proper conductor sizing and protection, short-circuit current rating (SCCR) calculated and labelled, lockout/tagout points, proper grounding and bonding, and panel construction meeting UL 50 for the enclosure.

SBKJ control panels destined for the US market are built to UL 508A standard with UL or cUL-listed components throughout — Schneider, Siemens, Allen-Bradley, ABB or Mitsubishi for contactors, drives and circuit protection. The panel carries a UL 508A label visible to the AHJ inspector. Short-circuit current rating is calculated for each panel and labelled per UL 508A SB4. Arc-flash labels are applied per NFPA 70E.

NEMA enclosure ratings

NEMA enclosure ratings classify cabinet protection against environmental ingress. The two most common ratings on US duct fabrication shop floors are NEMA 12 (dust-tight, drip-tight, indoor non-washdown) and NEMA 4 (watertight, washdown, suitable for indoor or outdoor). NEMA 12 is the default for typical sheet-metal shops with HVAC-conditioned air, dry processes and minimal coolant. NEMA 4 or NEMA 4X is specified where the shop has significant cutting fluid mist, washdown cleaning, or outdoor-adjacent locations.

SBKJ standard for North America is NEMA 12 control enclosure with NEMA 4-rated push-button stations and remote junction boxes where they are exposed to coolant or coil-handling debris. NEMA 4 control enclosure is available as a project option and adds approximately 6–10 percent to the panel cost.

OSHA 29 CFR 1910 machine guarding

OSHA — the Occupational Safety and Health Administration — enforces 29 CFR 1910 Subpart O (machine guarding) on every powered industrial machine in a US workplace. The relevant requirements for an auto duct line or coil line include interlocked guards on all rotating and reciprocating components, two-hand controls or light curtains on press operations, emergency stop within reach of every operator station, lockout/tagout procedures and points, nip-point guards on all rolling and forming stations, and high-visibility signage on all hazard zones.

SBKJ machines for the North American market are guarded per OSHA 29 CFR 1910 with interlocked perimeter guards, dual-channel safety relays, e-stops at every operator station, light curtains on the press-brake and shear stations where applicable, and lockout/tagout points clearly identified and labelled. The CE Machinery Directive guarding standard SBKJ already builds to is in most respects more stringent than OSHA, so the OSHA-compliance path is largely about labelling and documentation rather than rebuilding guards.

ETL or UL listing of complete machinery

For most US duct shop installations, UL 508A panel listing plus individually-listed UL/cUL components on the panel is sufficient for AHJ acceptance. A small number of jurisdictions and a small number of project specifications — typically federal government facilities, certain New York City projects, and some California state projects — require Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory (NRTL) listing on the complete machine assembly. The two most commonly accepted NRTLs are UL itself and Intertek (ETL listing).

SBKJ does not claim NRTL listing on complete machine assemblies as a default because the cost-to-benefit ratio is poor for the typical commercial duct shop. For projects where the AHJ specifically requires ETL or UL listing, SBKJ engages Intertek or UL on a project-specific basis and quotes the listing as a separate line item — typically USD 8,000–25,000 depending on machine complexity and how many units share a single listing.

7. Material sourcing — galvanized coil, stainless and US gauge thicknesses

The single largest variable cost in a US duct shop is coil. Sourcing it correctly — at the right gauge, the right galvanizing class, the right width, and from a domestic mill where Buy America applies — is half the operations job.

Galvanized coil — G60 vs G90

Hot-dip galvanized steel sheet for HVAC ductwork is specified by the galvanizing class, which describes the total zinc coating weight on both sides of the sheet. The two dominant classes are G60 (0.60 oz/ft² total both sides, ASTM A653) and G90 (0.90 oz/ft² total both sides). G60 is the default for indoor commercial duct in HVAC-conditioned environments — most office, retail and education duct is G60. G90 is specified for outdoor duct, exhaust duct exposed to higher humidity or condensation, and any project where the spec calls it out (some healthcare and laboratory specs default to G90 for corrosion margin).

US domestic galvanizing mills include Steel Dynamics, Nucor, Cleveland-Cliffs, US Steel and ArcelorMittal Dofasco. Most US duct shops source coil through service centres rather than direct from the mill — the largest service centres carry G60 and G90 in the most common widths and gauges on the floor. For a fabricator running 1,500 metres of duct per shift, expect to consume 35–55 tonnes of coil per month at typical gauge mix, or roughly 8–12 master coils per month from a single supplier.

Stainless 304 and 316L

Stainless steel duct is specified for healthcare exhaust, food-grade exhaust, laboratory fume exhaust, certain semiconductor process exhaust, and any project with corrosion or cleanability requirements that galvanized cannot meet. The two dominant grades are 304 (general food and pharmaceutical) and 316L (chemical, coastal, marine and aggressive industrial environments — the L denotes low carbon content for improved welding behaviour).

Stainless coil costs 4–7x galvanized on a per-pound basis depending on the nickel and molybdenum spot prices, so projects calling for stainless duct are budgeted very differently from galvanized. SBKJ auto duct lines and TDF flangers handle 304 and 316L without modification provided the coil thickness is within the machine's specified range; tooling life on stainless is typically 60–70 percent of the metres-formed life on galvanized at the same gauge.

US gauge thicknesses

The US sheet-metal gauge system is non-linear and worth memorising for any fabricator specifying coil. The conversion to decimal inches and millimetres for galvanized sheet (the values for stainless are slightly different but close enough for fabrication purposes) is:

  • 26 ga = 0.0179 in. = 0.45 mm
  • 24 ga = 0.0276 in. = 0.70 mm
  • 22 ga = 0.0336 in. = 0.85 mm
  • 20 ga = 0.0396 in. = 1.00 mm
  • 18 ga = 0.0516 in. = 1.31 mm
  • 16 ga = 0.0598 in. = 1.52 mm
  • 14 ga = 0.0785 in. = 1.99 mm

SBKJ auto duct lines for the US market are specified to handle the full 26 ga to 16 ga range as standard, with 14 ga capability available as a heavy-duty option for industrial duct shops. The SMACNA gauge chart reference is the day-to-day lookup most US fabricators use to confirm gauge selection against pressure class and duct dimension.

Coil width standardisation

US coil widths are typically supplied in 36 in., 48 in., 60 in. and occasionally 72 in., or in the metric equivalents of 1,200 mm, 1,250 mm, 1,300 mm, 1,500 mm and 1,524 mm. Slit coil to project-specific widths is available from most service centres at modest premiums. Specifying the auto duct line for the widest coil you reasonably expect to run — 60 in. / 1,524 mm is the typical maximum on commercial duct lines — preserves flexibility. SBKJ auto duct lines accept coil widths to specification, with the SBAL-V series sized for 1,500 mm coil as the default North American specification.

8. US labour costs and automation ROI

The single biggest economic driver pushing US duct shops from manual lockformer-and-Pittsburgh production to integrated auto duct lines is labour. US sheet-metal worker wages — combining base pay, fringe benefits, payroll taxes and workers' compensation — run USD 65–110 per loaded hour on union jobs in major metros (NYC, Bay Area, Chicago, LA, Boston) and USD 40–75 per loaded hour on non-union jobs in lower-cost regions (Texas, Southeast, Mountain West). Skilled fitters and lead hands run higher.

A traditional Pittsburgh-and-stomper shop produces 200–400 metres of finished rectangular duct per fitter per shift. A single auto duct line with two operators produces 1,000–2,000 metres per shift. The labour leverage is roughly 5–8x — and that arithmetic only gets stronger as US labour costs have continued to escalate at 4–7 percent per year through the 2020s.

Worked ROI — single-line shop

Take a mid-tier US duct shop running 1,500 metres of duct per shift on a Pittsburgh-and-stomper layout, with eight fitters at USD 65/hour loaded, working an 8-hour shift. Daily labour cost is 8 × 8 × 65 = USD 4,160/day, or roughly USD 1.04 million per year (250 production days). The same 1,500 metres of duct on a single SBKJ SBAL-V auto duct line with two operators is 2 × 8 × 65 = USD 1,040/day, or USD 260,000 per year. Annual labour saving: USD 780,000.

SBAL-V auto duct line landed cost in the US — machine FOB Melbourne, ocean freight, US import duty under HS 8462.49 (typically 0–4.4 percent for non-Section 301 origin), drayage, rigging and installation supervision — is in the USD 320,000–480,000 range depending on configuration. Payback on labour alone is 5–8 months. Add reduced rework, lower coil scrap, faster delivery (winning more bids on schedule), and the IRR on the capital outlay is generally 80–120 percent.

The arithmetic is what drives the tier 2 to tier 1 transition in the US fabrication industry. The ROI conversation is not subtle — once a shop owner commits a few hours to honest pricing, the auto duct line decision is straightforward. The harder decisions are coil-line vs single-line, twin-line vs single-line, and what spiral tubeformer to add for round duct.

Coil-line and second-line decisions

A single-line shop running 1,500–2,000 metres per shift typically runs into a capacity ceiling around 75–80 percent line utilisation, where small upsets propagate quickly. The natural next step is one of three options: add a coil line front-end (decoiler-leveller-cut-to-length-stacker) feeding the auto duct line, add a second auto duct line in parallel, or add a spiral tubeformer for round duct that frees up auto duct line capacity for rectangular work. The right choice depends on the project mix; SBKJ engineers walk customers through all three with a one-page workbook of numbers, and most tier-2 shops sequence a coil-line front-end first, then a spiral tubeformer, then a second auto duct line.

9. Logistics — Australia to US ports

SBKJ machinery destined for the United States is consolidated in Melbourne and shipped from the Port of Melbourne via the major Asia-Pacific to North America container services. The five US ports of entry SBKJ ships into most often, with typical transit times and notes, are:

  • Port of Los Angeles / Long Beach (LA/LB) — the dominant US West Coast entry point. Port of Melbourne to LA/LB is typically 22–28 days. Onward drayage to most California, Arizona, Nevada and Utah destinations is 1–3 days. Customs clearance through a competent US broker is normally 24–72 hours after vessel arrival.
  • Port of Oakland — Northern California entry. 22–28 days from Melbourne. Onward drayage to Bay Area and Sacramento Valley destinations is same-day or next-day.
  • Port of Houston — Gulf Coast entry, transits via the Panama Canal. 32–38 days from Melbourne. Houston is the natural entry for Texas, Louisiana, Oklahoma, and the lower Mississippi River corridor.
  • Port of Savannah — Southeast entry, fastest growing US port for industrial machinery. 38–44 days from Melbourne. Savannah serves Georgia, the Carolinas, Tennessee, Alabama and Florida via inland trucking.
  • Port of New York / New Jersey — Northeast entry. 38–44 days from Melbourne. NY/NJ serves the entire I-95 corridor from DC to Boston.

Container choice for SBKJ machinery is typically a 40-foot high-cube (40' HC) container for single auto duct lines under 25 tonnes, with break-bulk or flat-rack for heavier equipment or for installations where the machine is too long for a standard container envelope. Crating is fumigated wood with ISPM-15 stamp — non-compliant crating is impounded by US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) on arrival and the buyer pays demurrage at port-storage rates while the issue is resolved.

Marine insurance is either supplied by SBKJ on CIF terms (all-risk cargo cover including general average) or arranged by the buyer on FOB terms. SBKJ does not ship a six-figure machine without all-risk cargo cover regardless of which party holds the policy — uninsured ocean transit on industrial machinery is a documented loss-event we will not allow on a customer's account.

For a deeper walk-through of import logistics, customs documentation and Incoterm selection, see the companion Importing HVAC Duct Machinery Internationally guide.

10. US import duties, HS classification and Section 301

HVAC duct forming machinery imported into the US is most commonly classified under one of two HTSUS codes:

  • HS 8462.49 — Other bending, folding, straightening or flattening machines for working metal. This is the most common classification for press-brake-style and roll-forming-style duct fabrication equipment.
  • HS 8479.89 — Other machines and mechanical appliances having individual functions. Used for integrated auto duct lines and spiral tubeformers that do not fit cleanly under 8462.

Most-favoured-nation duty rates on these HTSUS codes are typically in the 0–4.4 percent range, depending on the precise sub-classification. SBKJ machinery shipped from Australia is a non-Section 301 origin, which means it is not subject to the additional Section 301 tariffs the US imposes on goods of certain other origins. SBKJ engineers do not give legal advice on tariff classification; engage a US-licensed customs broker for any project-specific HTSUS determination.

The customs broker handles entry filing, duty payment, ISF (Importer Security Filing) submission 24 hours before vessel loading, CBP clearance and any FDA/EPA/USDA referrals if applicable. 11. Certification pathways — AHRI, ETL, UL and AHJ acceptance

The certification landscape for HVAC machinery in the US confuses many first-time importers. Three observations make it manageable:

First, AHRI does not certify duct fabrication machinery. AHRI (the Air-Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration Institute) certifies HVAC equipment performance — chillers, air handlers, packaged units, terminal devices — against AHRI standards. Duct fabrication machinery is industrial sheet-metal forming equipment and falls outside AHRI's certification scope.

Second, UL 508A panel listing is the workhorse. For the vast majority of US AHJ inspections of duct fabrication machinery, what the inspector is looking for is the UL 508A panel listing label on the control cabinet plus individual UL/cUL listings on the major electrical components. SBKJ ships machinery to North America with this configuration as standard.

Third, ETL or UL listing of complete machinery is project-specific. A small subset of US installations — typically federal facilities, certain NYC projects, and some California state buildings — require NRTL listing on the complete machine. UL and Intertek (ETL) are the two most commonly accepted NRTLs. SBKJ engages either on a project-specific basis when required and quotes the listing as a separate scope.

For the typical commercial sheet-metal shop in Texas, Georgia, Tennessee, Arizona, North Carolina or any of the dozens of states without unusual project-specific requirements, the UL 508A panel listing plus individually-listed components is sufficient and is the SBKJ default.

12. Buy America and Build America considerations

Federal infrastructure projects funded through the Inflation Reduction Act, CHIPS Act, Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and various agency programmes carry Buy America (BABA — Build America, Buy America Act) requirements. BABA requires that iron and steel, manufactured products and construction materials used in covered federal projects be produced in the United States. The threshold for "manufactured product" is generally that 55 percent of cost is US content, though waivers and exclusions apply.

For an HVAC duct fabrication shop, BABA applies in two distinct ways. On the inputs side, the coil that becomes duct on a covered federal project must be from a US mill — most US service centres carry domestic G60 and G90 coil and can certify origin. On the equipment side, the question of whether the auto duct line itself is "construction material" is generally answered no — the equipment is shop equipment, not building material — but project-specific BABA determinations should be confirmed by the project's compliance officer.

SBKJ is happy to provide origin documentation for any project requiring it, and on Buy Clean projects we can supply environmental product declarations and embodied carbon data on the machinery itself. For BABA-covered projects where the duct produced on the line is the regulated item, the relevant compliance document is the coil mill certificate, not the machine origin.

13. Operator training and maintenance in US shops

The single biggest contributor to first-year machine availability in a US duct shop is operator training. Three observations from commissioning more than 5,000 SBKJ machines globally:

First, the operator who runs the line on day one should also do the maintenance during the first six months. The mechanical-feel knowledge an operator builds in the first 2,000 hours on the machine is irreplaceable for diagnosing developing issues — and a new operator coming in cold to a 12-month-old machine cannot replicate it. SBKJ training is delivered to the named operator and named maintenance lead — not to a rotating cohort.

Second, the maintenance schedule must be calendarised on day one and signed off weekly. The most common US duct shop machine failure pattern is "the machine is producing fine, so we postponed the 200-hour service for 600 hours, and now the bearing is gone." Discipline on the schedule is more important than depth of skill on any individual service. SBKJ ships every machine with a spreadsheet maintenance log calibrated to your shift hours.

Third, English-language documentation is the floor. Every SBKJ machine ships with operator manual, maintenance manual, electrical drawings, hydraulic and pneumatic schematics, PLC programme backup on USB, and a one-page quick-reference card in English by default. SBKJ commissioning engineers are bilingual English speakers; training sessions, FAT reports and warranty correspondence are conducted in English.

Spare parts strategy

Lead times on stocked SBKJ spares to North America are typically 7–14 days by airfreight (DHL, FedEx, UPS) from Melbourne, with Saturday delivery available at premium. FAQ

What is the size of the United States HVAC duct fabrication market?

The US HVAC ductwork installation market is conservatively in the USD 18–22 billion per year range. Sheet-metal duct accounts for roughly 75 percent of rectangular and round duct installed in commercial buildings. The fabrication side is a fragmented industry with several thousand HVAC sheet-metal contractors operating fabrication shops across the country, ranging from single-lockformer regional shops to multi-line auto-duct fabricators serving national general contractors.

Which standards govern HVAC duct construction in the US?

The dominant fabrication standard is the SMACNA HVAC Duct Construction Standards 4th Edition. Code-side, the IMC is adopted in most states, NFPA 90A and 90B govern fire protection, ASHRAE 90.1-2022 governs energy and duct leakage, IECC governs duct insulation and residential leakage, and UL 181 covers duct sealants and closure systems. California adds Title 24, Florida overlays HVHZ, and New York City has its own amendments.

What electrical specification is required for US duct machinery?

480V 60Hz three-phase is standard. UL 508A control panel listing is the workhorse certification, with NEMA 12 enclosures for typical shop floors and NEMA 4 for washdown. OSHA 29 CFR 1910 machine guarding compliance is mandatory. SBKJ ships machines configured for North America with all of the above as standard.

How long does shipping take from Australia to a US port?

Production lead time 75–90 days from order. Ocean transit Melbourne to LA/LB or Oakland 22–28 days, to Houston 32–38 days via Panama Canal, to Savannah or NY/NJ 38–44 days. Add 7–14 days for US Customs and drayage. Total order-to-shop-floor: 110–150 days West Coast, 130–170 days Gulf or East Coast.

Are SBKJ machines subject to Section 301 tariffs?

No. SBKJ machinery is shipped from Australia and is a non-Section 301 origin. HTSUS classification is typically 8462.49 or 8479.89 with most-favoured-nation duty rates in the 0–4.4 percent range. Engage a US-licensed customs broker for project-specific tariff determination.
