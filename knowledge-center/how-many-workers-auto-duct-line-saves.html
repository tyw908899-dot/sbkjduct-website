How Much Labor Can an Auto Duct Line Save?

A factual comparison of manual duct production staffing versus SBAL-V automated line operation — with output differences, productivity per operator, and a realistic framework for evaluating the investment.

Honest caveat upfront: Labor savings vary significantly by market, wage level, management quality, and product mix. This guide gives you the analytical framework — not a single promised number. Use it as a structure for calculating your own scenario.

Manual Rectangular Duct Production: Typical Staffing

A well-organized manual duct production operation producing 300–400 m²/day typically requires:

Total9–11 workersPer shift for 300–400 m²/day

At higher outputs (600–800 m²/day), manual operations typically require 14–18 workers per shift and face compounding coordination problems — material handling delays, variation between workers, and quality inconsistency across shifts.

A Simplified Payback Framework

This framework helps you estimate a rough payback period for your specific labor market. It is not a financial guarantee — use it to test whether automation makes sense for your situation before requesting a quote.

Step 1: Calculate your current labor cost per m²

Current daily labor cost (wages × workers) ÷ current daily output (m²) = labor cost per m²

Step 2: Estimate automated labor cost per m²

Automated daily labor cost (wages × 3 workers) ÷ automated daily output (m²) = automated labor cost per m²

Step 3: Calculate labor saving per m²

Manual labor cost per m² − automated labor cost per m² = saving per m²

Step 4: Estimate annual saving

Saving per m² × annual production volume (m²) = annual labor saving

Step 5: Rough payback

Machine investment ÷ annual saving = payback in years

Example calculation (illustrative only):
Current: 10 workers × $25/day = $250/day labor for 350 m²/day = $0.71/m²
Automated: 3 workers × $25/day = $75/day labor for 1,200 m²/day = $0.063/m²
Saving: $0.71 − $0.063 = $0.65/m²
Annual volume: 365,000 m²/year
Annual labor saving: $237,000
Payback (on labor alone): ~3–4 years depending on machine price

This does not include material waste reduction (~3–5% saving), rework reduction, or the revenue from selling additional capacity. With those included, realistic payback is typically 2–3 years in moderate-wage markets.

What the Calculation Doesn't Include

The labor cost saving above understates the total economic case for automation:

  • Material waste reduction: Manual production typically runs 4–7% scrap rate. CNC-controlled auto lines reduce this to under 1.5%. At steel prices of $700–$1,200/ton, this adds up quickly at volume.
  • Rework and rejection reduction: Consistent ±0.5mm tolerance means fewer field rejections, fewer re-fabrication jobs, and less customer dissatisfaction.
  • Additional capacity: The SBAL-V can produce 800–2,500 m²/day. If your current market can absorb more volume, the line enables revenue growth that manual production cannot match.
  • Double-shift capability: With only 2–3 operators needed, running a second shift is economically viable. Manual operations rarely justify second-shift economics.

When Automation Is Not the Right Decision Yet

Not every factory is ready for a full auto duct line. Automation makes sense when:

  • Your daily output requirement is consistently above 400–500 m²/day
  • You have stable long-term order volume (not project-by-project uncertainty)
  • You have floor space and utility infrastructure for the line
  • You can operate the line for at least one full shift per day

If your current volume is 200–350 m²/day with significant uncertainty, the SBAL-III (lower output, lower investment) may be a better starting point than the SBAL-V — or individual forming machines may be more appropriate than a full auto line.

Discuss this honestly with Ryan before committing. A line that is oversized for your current volume is not a good investment even if the long-term case is sound.
